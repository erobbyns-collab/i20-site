NHS Dental Deserts: Inflation Hits 3.6% Amid Care Access Crisis

Data refreshed 23 October 2026, 17:08 UTC

The recent announcement from the Office for National Statistics revealing a Consumer Prices Index including owner occupiers' housing costs (CPIH) inflation rate of 3.6% for September 2026 has significant implications for the already precarious state of dental care access across the UK. As inflation continues to rise, so too does the cost of living, which disproportionately affects lower-income families who are struggling to afford basic healthcare services. In this context, the concept of NHS dental deserts—areas lacking adequate access to dental care—becomes even more pronounced, exacerbating health inequalities that have persisted for decades.

The challenges posed by these dental deserts are multifaceted. Areas deemed 'dental deserts' often coincide with regions experiencing high levels of deprivation, where residents are increasingly unable to find NHS dentists accepting new patients. The combination of rising inflation and stagnant wages means that more individuals are opting to forgo regular dental check-ups, leading to a cycle of poor oral health that can have far-reaching consequences. The increased financial burden of dental care services, which are already notoriously underfunded, tends to push many families toward private options or to forgo treatment altogether, thus worsening their health outcomes.

Compounding these difficulties, the rise in inflation can affect the operational costs of dental practices themselves, including expenses for materials and staffing. Many practitioners may find it increasingly difficult to maintain their NHS contracts, leading to an exodus from NHS dentistry into more lucrative private practices. As a result, the widening gap between those who can afford dental care and those who cannot threatens to create a two-tier dental system, where only affluent communities enjoy the benefits of comprehensive oral health services. This not only raises ethical concerns but also places additional strain on the NHS, which is already grappling with a backlog of services due to the pandemic.

As society grapples with these inflationary pressures, it is crucial to consider the long-term implications for public health policy. The government's response to this crisis will play a pivotal role in determining whether access to dental care becomes a privilege reserved for the wealthy or a fundamental right for all. Urgent action is needed to address the disparity in service provision and to ensure that dental care is not sidelined in the broader conversation about health equity. With inflation steering conversations towards economic recovery, it is imperative that policymakers recognise the interconnectedness of economic and health outcomes, ensuring that all individuals have access to the dental care they require to lead healthy lives.
