Digital-Only Government Services Leave 20.9% of Young Adults Behind

Data refreshed 02 November 2026, 13:57 UTC

In a rapidly digitising society, the UK government’s shift towards digital-only services raises serious concerns about inclusivity, particularly for vulnerable groups. With the latest statistic revealing an economic inactivity rate of 20.9% among those aged 16 to 64, it is crucial to examine how this trend may exacerbate existing inequalities. Many of those who fall into this statistic are not just unemployed; they may be facing barriers such as disability, lack of digital literacy, or simply the challenges of navigating a tech-driven world.

The implications of a digital-first approach extend beyond mere inconvenience; they touch upon fundamental rights and access to essential services. Citizens often rely on government services for vital support, such as healthcare, benefits, and housing assistance. As these services migrate online, the risk of alienating those without reliable internet access or the skills to use digital platforms becomes starkly apparent. This digital divide is not merely a technological issue but a pressing social concern that can impact mental health and economic stability for many individuals and families.

Moreover, the reliance on digital systems can inadvertently reinforce existing socio-economic disparities. Those who are economically inactive may lack the resources to adapt to this new landscape, further entrenching their marginalisation. It is essential to consider that the 20.9% figure is not just a statistic; it represents real people facing real challenges. The move towards digital services must be accompanied by strategies that address these barriers, ensuring that no one is left behind in an increasingly digital world.

The future of government services must involve a balance between innovation and accessibility. As we venture deeper into the digital age, policymakers must prioritise inclusive practices that cater to all citizens, particularly the most vulnerable. This includes investing in digital literacy programmes, offering offline alternatives, and ensuring that robust support systems are in place to assist those struggling with the transition. Only then can we hope to create a society where digital advancements enhance, rather than hinder, the well-being of every individual in the UK.
