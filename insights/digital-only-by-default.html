Digital-Only Services Risk Leaving 20.9% of Young Adults Behind

Data refreshed 23 October 2026, 17:08 UTC

As the UK government continues its push towards digital-only services, a staggering 20.9% of individuals aged 16 to 64 are classified as economically inactive, according to the latest data from the Office for National Statistics. This figure, which reflects a growing trend of disengagement from the labour market, raises critical questions about the inclusivity of government initiatives aimed at streamlining services. Are we inadvertently alienating those who most need support by transitioning to a purely digital landscape?

The implications of this statistic are particularly pronounced for vulnerable groups who may lack access to technology or the necessary digital literacy skills to navigate online platforms. Many among the economically inactive population are young adults facing barriers such as mental health issues, disabilities, or family responsibilities. For them, the shift to digital-only services could exacerbate existing inequalities and deepen their sense of isolation. With government services already challenging to access for those with limited mobility or cognitive challenges, moving these services online could effectively cut off vital support systems.

Historically, the UK has prided itself on being a leader in social welfare; however, the current trajectory suggests a departure from these principles. The move towards digitalisation is often justified by efficiency and cost-cutting measures, yet these benefits may be overshadowed by the potential harm to the most vulnerable. A society is only as strong as its commitment to ensuring that all individuals can participate fully, both economically and socially. By neglecting those who are economically inactive, we risk entrenching a two-tier system where access to essential services hinges on one's ability to engage with technology.

As we look to the future, the challenge will be to strike a balance between technological advancement and social responsibility. The government must consider alternative solutions that incorporate both digital and traditional service delivery methods. Community outreach initiatives, digital literacy programmes, and hybrid service models could help bridge the gap, ensuring that no one is left behind. By doing so, the UK can foster a more inclusive society that empowers all its citizens, regardless of their economic activity status, to thrive in an increasingly digital world.
