Digital-Only Government Services Risk Leaving 20.8% Behind

Data refreshed 23 November 2026, 16:37 UTC

In a rapidly digitising world, the UK government’s push towards digital-only services raises urgent concerns, particularly for those who may be left behind. With the latest data revealing an economic inactivity rate of 20.8% among individuals aged 16 to 64, it is crucial to examine how this transition can exacerbate existing inequalities. As public services increasingly pivot to online platforms, those who are economically inactive—whether due to health issues, caregiving responsibilities, or lack of digital skills—face significant barriers to accessing essential services.

The implications of this statistic extend beyond mere inconvenience. Vulnerable groups, including the elderly, individuals with disabilities, and those without reliable internet access, are at risk of becoming further marginalised. The digital divide in the UK is not just about access to technology; it encompasses a broader spectrum of socio-economic factors that hinder equal participation in society. For instance, a significant portion of the economically inactive population may lack the technical skills necessary to navigate online systems, thus making it exceedingly difficult to apply for benefits, access healthcare information, or engage with local government initiatives. This scenario raises the question: what happens to those who cannot transition to a digital-first model?

Evidence from various studies highlights that government services are not only a lifeline for those in need but also a barometer of social equity. A sizeable portion of the population relies on face-to-face interactions for assistance, guidance, and reassurance. The shift to digital-only services could inadvertently criminalise the vulnerability of these individuals. Local councils and service providers must consider how their policies impact not only the digitally literate but also those who are economically inactive. The government's digital transformation strategy must incorporate robust outreach initiatives that bridge the gap for those unable to adapt to the online environment. Without these measures, the risk of disenfranchisement grows, leading to a society that is increasingly divided along lines of technological access and capability.

As the government forges ahead with its digital agenda, it is imperative to foster an inclusive approach that encourages participation rather than exclusion. The challenge lies not only in the technological implementation but in cultivating a culture that recognises the diverse needs of the population. Policymakers should engage with community organisations and leverage existing networks to ensure that all groups are considered in the transition to digital services. By prioritising accessibility and support for the economically inactive, the UK can move towards a more equitable future, where every citizen, regardless of their circumstances, has the opportunity to thrive in a digital landscape.
