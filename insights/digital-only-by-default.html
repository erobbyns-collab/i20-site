Digital-Only Government Services Risk Excluding 20.9% of UK Workforce

Data refreshed 26 October 2026, 11:39 UTC

The recent statistic revealing a 20.9% economic inactivity rate among 16 to 64-year-olds in the UK presents a troubling backdrop for the government's push towards digital-only services. As more public services transition online, the implications for a significant portion of the population are profound. This figure, sourced from the Office for National Statistics, highlights the challenges faced by those who are disengaged from the labour market—many of whom may also struggle with digital literacy, access to technology, or even the motivation to engage with online platforms.

Among the groups most affected are the elderly, disabled individuals, and those from disadvantaged socio-economic backgrounds. These demographics often rely on face-to-face interactions with government services, whether for job-seeking support, health services, or social benefits. The move towards a fully digital approach raises legitimate concerns about exacerbating inequalities. If these services are only available online, individuals who lack the necessary skills or resources may find themselves increasingly marginalised, unable to access essential support at a time when they need it most.

The potential fallout from this shift could lead to a cycle of exclusion, where those already facing barriers to economic participation are further alienated. This is particularly pertinent given the current economic climate, where many are grappling with job insecurity and rising living costs. The reliance on technology not only risks sidelining vulnerable groups but also places additional pressure on those who might be struggling to adapt. In a society that prides itself on inclusivity, the digitisation of services without adequate consideration for all demographics may inadvertently reinforce existing disparities, leaving the most vulnerable to fend for themselves.

As policymakers consider the future of government services, there is an urgent need to balance innovation with accessibility. A hybrid model that incorporates both digital and in-person options could provide a solution, ensuring that while the government embraces technological advancements, it does not alienate those who are unable to navigate this new landscape. The challenge lies in designing services that are not only efficient but also equitable, ensuring that no one is left behind in the digital revolution. The statistic of 20.9% serves as a stark reminder that as we advance into a more digital future, inclusivity must remain at the forefront of governmental planning and execution.
